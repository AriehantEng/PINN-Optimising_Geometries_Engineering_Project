[Step 1 · Establish a territory]

Branching channel networks are among the most widespread structures in engineering and biology, from the vascular system to the manifolds that distribute fluid across a lab-on-a-chip (Emerson et al., 2006; Murray, 1926a). The classical rule for sizing them dates to 1926, when Murray showed that minimising the combined cost of pumping blood and maintaining its volume fixes the size of every daughter vessel relative to its parent (Murray, 1926a; Stephenson et al., 2015). The rule, named Murray's law by Sherman (1981, as cited in Hagmeijer & Venner, 2018), has since moved well beyond physiology. It is used to set channel widths in microfluidic manifolds (Emerson et al., 2006; Stephenson et al., 2015) and to design synthetic porous materials for mass transport in catalysis, sensing and energy applications (Zhou et al., 2024). Its relevance has grown with inertial microfluidics, which deliberately operates at channel Reynolds numbers between roughly 1 and 100 to focus, concentrate and separate particles at high throughput (Zhang et al., 2016).

Since then, Murray's analysis has been extended well beyond its original setting. Emerson et al. (2006) generalised the law to the constant-depth rectangular and trapezoidal channels typical of microfabricated devices, and Stephenson et al. (2015) extended it to channels of arbitrary cross-section and scale, including constant-depth networks. For planar channels whose width varies at fixed depth, both Stephenson et al.'s and Zhou et al.'s frameworks give a square law, w₀² = w₁² + w₂², rather than Murray's cube law (Stephenson et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2024), which Zhou et al. (2024) confirmed experimentally at fixed total volume. Equivalently, at fixed total volume each daughter's optimal width scales with the square root of the flow it carries, w ∝ Q^(1/2), whatever the channel lengths (Appendix A.2). Wechsatol et al. (2006) incorporated junction losses through loss-coefficient models and showed that they can reshape the optimal geometry of compact tree networks, and Hagmeijer and Venner (2018) established the necessary and sufficient conditions under which Murray-type laws give minimum power.

In parallel, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) have emerged as a mesh-free way to solve the equations that govern such flows. A PINN represents the solution as a neural network trained to satisfy the governing equations at scattered points, with derivatives computed by automatic differentiation (Cai et al., 2021; Raissi et al., 2019). PINNs trained only on boundary conditions, with no interior or pressure data, have solved the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations (Jin et al., 2021). Because the network is differentiable with respect to its inputs (Raissi et al., 2019), a geometric parameter can itself be made an input, so that a single trained network represents an entire family of designs (Gammaidoni et al., 2026; Hennigh et al., 2021; L. Sun et al., 2020). Recent studies have built on this to perform inverse design and shape optimisation with PINNs (Hao et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2021; Y. Sun et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), and PINNs have also simulated laminar flow in three-dimensional Y-shaped mixers (Sedykh et al., 2024).

[Step 2 · Establish a niche]

However, the Murray-type design rules above share one assumption: that each channel carries fully developed flow and that the junction itself dissipates no energy (Hagmeijer & Venner, 2018; Stephenson et al., 2015). The fully developed theory does relate the widths to the branch angles and to unequal flows: Murray (1926b) applied the same minimum-work principle to the angles at which vessels branch, and Zamir (1978) extended it to non-symmetrical bifurcations. But because the junction costs nothing in that theory, the angles can enter only through the channel lengths they imply (Appendix A.2). Where the junction flow has been resolved, it has been in particular layouts. Emerson et al.'s (2006) test of Murray-based predictions used a square-channel manifold of T-junctions and 90° bends: the predicted flow resistance was within 2% for inlet Reynolds numbers up to 25, but about 6% low at Re = 50 and about 30% low at Re = 250, owing to secondary eddies at the bends. Bejan et al. (2000) optimised the geometry of T- and Y-shaped constructs of fluid streams, Wechsatol et al. (2006) represented junction losses with empirical coefficients rather than resolving the junction flow, and topology optimisation finds a single optimal layout for given conditions, in Stokes flow (Borrvall & Petersson, 2003) and at finite Reynolds number (Gersborg-Hansen et al., 2005). [Read Murray (1926b), Zamir (1978), Bejan et al. (2000), Borrvall & Petersson (2003) and Gersborg-Hansen et al. (2005) before submission, and adjust these sentences to what they say.] To our knowledge, no study has mapped how the departure of the optimal widths from Murray's rule depends on the branch angles and on the share of the flow each daughter must carry, with the junction flow resolved. In a manifold, the angles and the flows are usually set by where the outlets must go and what they must deliver; the width split is the variable left to the designer, and the one this study maps.

A second shortcoming concerns the tools. While PINNs have been extensively studied as flow solvers (Cai et al., 2021; Raissi et al., 2019), to our knowledge their value inside a design loop, where they must locate an optimum rather than reproduce a flow, has rarely been measured against an independent solver. For a single forward solve, PINNs have not outperformed the finite-element method on accuracy or solution time in the benchmark problems tested (Grossmann et al., 2024). Most machine-learning studies of fluid problems that claim to beat numerical solvers compare against weak baselines (McGreivy & Hakim, 2024), and PINNs trained without data have converged to non-physical solutions, missing vortex shedding entirely (Chuang & Barba, 2022). Where a parametric PINN has been costed against repeated solves for a family of microchannel geometries, it repaid its training only after about 21 geometries (Gammaidoni et al., 2026). [Confirm this figure in Gammaidoni et al. (2026).] An optimum predicted by a PINN therefore has to be checked with an independent solve (Lu et al., 2021; Mowlavi & Nabi, 2023).

Optimisation is, moreover, a different test from simulation. Near an optimum the objective is flat (Hagmeijer & Venner, 2018): for the bifurcation studied here, at equal shares, a 0.01 error in the width split raises the pressure drop by only about 0.2% (Appendix A.3). A design can therefore be nearly optimal in cost while visibly wrong in geometry, and the location of the minimum then depends on the slope of the predicted objective rather than its value: an error in the pressure drop that changes by 1% across a 0.1 change in split moves the predicted optimum by about 0.002 (Appendix A.3). Because network-based optimisers have rarely been tested on a problem whose answer is known, the difference between these two kinds of error, in the design and in its cost, has gone largely unmeasured.

[Step 3 · Occupy the niche]

The purpose of this investigation is to address both gaps with one design problem that has a known answer in one limit and an unknown one in general. A parent channel splits into two daughters of equal length that leave at angles α₁ and α₂ from its axis, each anywhere from 0° to 180°, so that both the bifurcation angle α₁ + α₂ and its orientation vary (Section 2). The daughters share the parent's width, w₁ + w₂ = w₀. Neither may be narrower than a tenth of the parent, and neither may the opening through which it is entered, measured from the parent's corner to the septum (Section 2). Each daughter must deliver a set share of the flow, q = Q₁/Q₀ for daughter 1, and the only free design variable is the width split s = w₁/w₀. The objective is the flux-weighted total-pressure drop, the pumping power per unit flow (Appendix A.6).

Two features define the problem. First, the shares must be set. With equal outlet pressures the daughters act as parallel resistances, and the pressure drop falls monotonically toward the narrowest branch allowed, which then carries almost none of the flow (Appendix A.1): 0.1–0.2% of it in this study (Section 4). Second, in the fully developed limit the problem has an exact answer. Minimising the daughters' dissipation under w₁ + w₂ = w₀ gives w₁/w₂ = (L₁/L₂)^(1/4) (Q₁/Q₂)^(1/2), which for daughters of equal length, as here, is the planar Murray split s_M = √q / (√q + √(1 − q)) (Appendix A.2). It provides a ground truth for the resolved optimum to depart from, and a baseline any network must beat to be worth training.

We work at Re_h = 10, based on the parent's half-width and centreline velocity. This corresponds to a hydraulic-diameter Reynolds number of about 27 (Appendix A.7), where Emerson et al. (2006) found Murray-based resistance predictions accurate to about 2%, so the departures mapped here come from the geometry of the junction rather than from inertia growing with flow rate. In two dimensions each daughter's Reynolds number is fixed by its share of the flow, whatever its width (Appendix A.7), so the design variable does not change the flow regime.

The approach links three networks. Network 1 resolves the developing, inertial flow in the parent channel and establishes how far upstream of the junction the inflow is developed. Network 2 is a physics-informed network for the flow through the bifurcation itself. A bifurcation makes two demands of it that a straight channel does not. Because the flow divides, an error in mass conservation becomes an error in the split itself: as Section 5 shows, a PINN that imposed continuity only as a penalty lost 6.3% of the inflow. And at the corners where the walls turn away from the flow, the exact velocity gradient is singular, so no smooth field can represent the flow there. With no-slip imposed through a wall-distance factor, as here, the equation residual even grows like the inverse distance to the corner, and residual-based training concentrates its error at those corners (Section 3). [Cite the corner singularity: Dean & Montagnon (1949) and Moffatt (1964); read both before citing.] Network 2 is therefore built so that no-slip, mass conservation and the prescribed shares hold exactly. As a result, a conservation check can no longer reveal its errors, and an energy-consistency check, which needs no reference data, becomes its only internal diagnostic (Appendix A.6). Network 3 is not physics-informed. It is trained on the finite-element optima, mapping the angles and the share to the optimal split and the lowest pressure drop. It learns only a correction to the Murray split and to the corresponding pressure drop, and by construction it cannot propose a width below the minimum. Every result is compared with a finite-element reference (Section 3). Its discretisation error is estimated by the grid convergence index in the channel (Celik et al., 2008), and in the bifurcation by re-solving every optimum on a finer mesh (Section 3).

This research addresses the following questions:

1. How far do the pressure-drop-optimal widths of a planar bifurcation depart from the planar Murray split when the junction flow is resolved, across flow shares, bifurcation angles and orientations?
2. Can neural networks replace the finite-element solver in this design loop, at equal accuracy and lower cost, and which parts of the loop can they take over?

We test three hypotheses:

* H1: where both daughters turn gently, the junction dissipates little and the optimal split follows the Murray split s_M to within the resolution of the reference (Appendix A.2).
* H2: where one daughter turns sharply relative to the other, the optimum departs from s_M toward a wider turned daughter, because the junction loss scales with that daughter's dynamic pressure (Appendix A.5).
* H3: a network's error in the optimal split is governed by its error in the slope of the predicted pressure drop, not in its value, so a design can carry a small pressure-drop penalty while its split is visibly in error (Appendix A.3).

[Impact]

The answer matters beyond pressure drop. In laminar Murray networks, the optimal design also makes wall shear stress uniform (Emerson et al., 2006; Hagmeijer & Venner, 2018), and Emerson et al. (2006) proposed using this to control the shear experienced by shear-sensitive cells. At the planar Murray split the two daughters carry the same wall shear (Appendix A.4); at the resolved optimum they do not. In this study the daughters' wall shear differs by up to 12% at bifurcation angles up to 90°, although the split there stays within 0.010 of s_M, because at small shares the shear ratio is very sensitive to the split: d ln(τ₁/τ₂) = −2 δs / [s(1 − s)] (Appendix A.4). When one daughter continues straight and the other turns back at 150°, the shear differs by a factor of 2.6 (Section 6). Designers of inertial devices therefore face a trade-off between minimum pumping power and equal shear that Murray's law conceals, in a field that still lacks quantitative design rules (Zhang et al., 2016). A network that proposes the optimal widths in milliseconds would make that trade-off cheap to explore. An honest negative result, showing where networks do not repay their cost, would be just as useful, as a warning against trusting unvalidated designs (Grossmann et al., 2024; McGreivy & Hakim, 2024).

In answering these questions, this study makes three contributions:

* A resolved design map at Re_h = 10: 165 finite-element optima over 15 layouts, with bifurcation angles from 30° to 180° and shares from 5% to 95% (Section 4). The optimal split stays within 0.010 of the Murray split for bifurcation angles up to 90° and within 0.006 at 180°. It departs by 0.023 at 120° and by 0.072 at 150° when one daughter continues straight, the turned daughter taking the extra width. Choosing the optimal split instead of equal widths lowers the pressure drop by up to 72%, and the orientation of a fixed bifurcation angle changes it by up to 23%.
* Three linked networks, each scored against the reference (Section 5). In the parent channel, Network 1 agrees with the reference to 3 × 10⁻⁶ in velocity and 4 × 10⁻⁶ in its pressure-drop objective (mean of three seeds), about four times and sixty times the reference's own estimated error (9 × 10⁻⁷ and 7 × 10⁻⁸); its pressure agrees to within the reference's error. Network 2 resolves the bifurcation with mass and shares exact and its pressure drop within 0.4%, but at more than 100 times the finite-element cost per geometry. Network 3 is trained on all 165 optima and tested on six further layouts, 18 cases each optimised separately by finite elements. Its proposed splits have a pressure drop within 0.016% of the optimum on average (0.077% at worst), about seven times closer than the Murray split, and it predicts the lowest pressure drop to within 0.5% (3% at worst).
* An equal-accuracy account of cost, following the fair-baseline rules of McGreivy and Hakim (2024): what each network costs to train and to query, and how much finite-element data it consumed (Section 6). It uses metrics that separate field accuracy from design accuracy: argmin error, design regret, and an energy-consistency check that needs no reference data (Appendix A.6).

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 formulates the design problem and derives the Murray split that serves as its ground truth. Section 3 describes the three networks and the finite-element reference. Section 4 presents the resolved optimum across shares, angles and orientations, answering the first research question (H1, H2). Section 5 evaluates the networks as solvers and as optimisers, answering the second (H3). Section 6 compares their cost with the finite-element route at equal accuracy and discusses the energy–shear trade-off, and Section 7 concludes and sets out the extension to higher Reynolds numbers, where inertia rather than geometry may drive the departure.
